Colors That Stop Matching

Two paints match in the shop, then clash under the lamps at home. Here is why.

Why the match breaks

It is natural to think that a paint simply has a color, the way it has a weight, and that light only shows it. Physics-education research finds that students keep this idea even after lessons on light (Haagen-Schützenhöfer, 2017). Pebble and Fog show why it cannot be the whole story: nothing about either paint changes when the lamp does, yet under one lamp they match and under the next they do not. Five small steps explain it, each with a demo you can play with.

1. Flip the lamp

Under the shop light the difference between Pebble and Fog is 0.0. Under the warm bulb it is 19.4, and in daylight 30.0. This page measures differences with CIEDE2000, a color-difference formula from the International Commission on Illumination (CIE). By a widely used rule of thumb, a difference of about 1 on its scale is just noticeable when two colors sit side by side, and a difference of 2 is plain to a careful eye, so 19 is hard to miss.

Two cans of the same paint would match under every lamp: press the button under the walls to try it. Only paints with different recipes can match under one lamp and split under another. To see why, we need to know what a paint's recipe is.

2. A color is a recipe of light

A paint gives out no light of its own. It sends back a share of the light that falls on it, and that share depends on the wavelength of the light, measured in nanometers (nm, billionths of a meter). One wavelength on its own looks like one color of the rainbow: violet at 400 nm, green near 530 nm, deep red at 700 nm. Most light mixes many wavelengths, and what you see depends on the whole mix. Plot the share against wavelength and you get the paint's reflectance curve: 0% means the paint swallows that light, 100% means it sends all of it back.

What reaches your eye is the lamp's light multiplied, wavelength by wavelength, by that curve. In the figure the top row is the lamp, the middle row is Pebble's curve, and the bottom row is their product: the light that actually leaves the paint. Drag the curve and the product and its color change at once.

Take one wavelength, 680 nm, a deep red. Pebble sends back 52% of it and Fog only 20%. For the same brightness on a white sheet, the warm bulb gives out about 17 times as much of that light as the shop light, so the same gap between Pebble and Fog counts about 17 times as much under the warm bulb.

3. Your eye keeps only three numbers

Color vision starts with three kinds of cone cell in your retina. Each kind adds up all the light that reaches it, favoring some wavelengths over others: one kind favors long wavelengths, one medium and one short. A whole curve of light becomes just three numbers, drawn here as three bars, each compared with what a white sheet under the same lamp would give.

So very different curves can produce the same three numbers. Color scientists call such look-alikes metamers, and the effect metamerism. Pebble and Fog are metamers under the shop light. At 700 nm Pebble sends back 62% of the light and Fog 11%; at 400 nm it is the other way round, 14% and 45%. Yet under the shop light both give 0.312, 0.306 and 0.300.

Strictly, a match holds for one particular eye. Everyone's cones differ a little, so two paints can match for you and not for the friend beside you; color scientists call this observer metamerism. Pebble and Fog were designed to match under the shop light for three of the CIE's model eyes at once (it calls them observers): What this page simplifies says which.

The shop light makes this easy. It gives out more than half of its light in three narrow lines, near 435, 545 and 610 nm. Half of what your eye gets from a white sheet under it comes from two thin slivers of the spectrum near 545 and 610 nm, 20 nm wide in all; under the warm bulb the same half is spread over 60 nm. Pebble and Fog come close at all three lines, never more than 6 points apart, and their curves cross seven times, once right beside the green line, at about 543 nm.

4. The invisible wiggle

Press Wiggle and Fog's curve thrashes while its color holds still. Every shape it passes through gives exactly the same three numbers under the chosen lamp, so to the standard eye this page uses they are all the same color.

Here is how. This page draws each curve through 13 handles, the dots you can drag in Make my own pair. Under the shop light no single handle can move unseen: lift any one of them and the numbers change. But the handles can move together, some up and some down, so that every kind of cone gains exactly as much light as it loses. Because the eye keeps three numbers, the handles can move together in 10 independent directions that stay hidden under one lamp, and only 3 directions show. At every frame the page takes a random change, removes the part the lamp would let you see, and keeps the rest; mathematicians call this a projection. Under the sodium street lamp your eye receives a single wavelength, so only one thing about the curve shows, its height at 589 nm, and 12 of the 13 directions stay hidden: choose Sodium lamp in the figure and watch that one point stay pinned.

Watch the second number while Fog wiggles: the same change that the shop light cannot see is plain under the warm bulb. The hidden directions belong to the lamp, not to the paint. In Make my own pair you can hold a match under several lamps at once. Each lamp you hold takes away three directions; hold the shop light, the warm bulb, daylight and the warm LED, and only one direction is left. Then only the two lamps this page models can still tell your paint from Pebble: the sodium street lamp, by at most about 3.4, and the smooth glow, by almost nothing.

5. The lamp lab: spiky lamps are harsher judges

A lamp that gives out its light in a few narrow peaks looks at a curve in only a few places. Pairs that differ elsewhere can match under it, and a match made under a smooth lamp can fall apart under it.

Four of the lamps here are what the CIE calls illuminants: standard tables of how much light a typical lamp gives out at each wavelength. A stands for a filament bulb, D65 for average daylight, FL11 for a three-band fluorescent tube and LED-B2 for a warm white LED. The other two are models made by this page. The number in kelvin (K) beside a lamp is its color temperature: an object heated to that temperature glows with light of about the same color, and the lower the number, the warmer the light. Planck's law gives the light of such a glowing object, so the smooth glow is the light of an object at 3999 K: smooth, and the same color as the shop light.

The lab tests this fairly. It makes 300 random pairs that match exactly under the warm bulb, then judges each pair under two lamps of the same color (they differ only in the fourth decimal): the shop light, with its three spiky lines, and the smooth glow. The shop light splits them more in about four pairs out of five, typically by about twice as much. Pebble and Fog themselves differ by 24.6 under the smooth glow, although it has the same color as the shop light that makes them match.

A low-pressure sodium street lamp is the extreme case. It puts nearly all of its light at 589 nm, in two lines 0.6 nm apart. Under it every paint can only send back its share of that one light, so colors all but vanish and little more than lightness is left. This page models the lamp as a single line; in that model two paints that send back the same share at 589 nm match exactly, however different they look by day. Under a real lamp they are very hard to tell apart.

Most white LED bulbs make their light with a blue chip under a yellow phosphor, a coating that glows yellow when blue light hits it; the CIE describes typical ones with illuminants named LED-B1 to LED-B5. Under LED-B2, a warm white, Pebble and Fog differ by 18.8.

Because every lamp judges differently, makers of paint, plastics and textiles check color matches in viewing booths that switch between simulated daylight, an incandescent lamp and a store lamp. A standard practice for judging colors by eye says that a match is usually confirmed under a very different light source.

Your screen is a metamer machine

Every swatch on this page is a fake. Your screen cannot copy Pebble's broad curve. It mixes three narrow bands of red, green and blue light, and a well-set-up screen chooses the amounts so that your three cone numbers come out as the paint's would. A swatch made that way is a metamer of the paint, not a copy of its light. This page aims for that in daylight; under the other lamps it keeps only part of each lamp's tint and gives every room the same brightness, as What this page simplifies explains.

You can see the trick for yourself. Look at a white patch with a magnifying glass or your phone camera's zoom, or put a tiny drop of water on a phone screen: you can see the red, green and blue dots that fake every color here. The figure's screen is a model with bands at 460, 532 and 618 nm; real screens differ in detail but work the same way.

Exercise 1: The street lamp

A low-pressure sodium street lamp gives out nearly all of its light at one wavelength, 589 nm. Paint P looks light blue by day and sends back 40% of 589 nm light. Paint Q looks mustard yellow by day and also sends back 40% at 589 nm. Under the street lamp, do they match?

Show the answer and the numbers

They match, in this page's model of the lamp: a single line at 589 nm. Each paint can only return its share of that one light, and both return 40% of it, so all three cone signals are equal. The difference is 0.0 under the street lamp, where both show as the same shade of orange, and 47.1 in daylight. A real lamp also gives out a little light at other wavelengths, so under it the two would be very hard, rather than impossible, to tell apart.

Exercise 2: Curves that never cross

Fabric R sends back more light than fabric S at every wavelength: R's curve lies above S's everywhere. Is there any lamp under which they match exactly, giving your eye the same three numbers?

Show the answer and the numbers

No lamp can. Whatever the lamp, R returns more of its light at every wavelength the lamp gives out, and the long- and medium-wavelength cones respond, if only a little, at every visible wavelength, so they always get more light from R. That is why two paints that match exactly must have curves that cross, or at least touch, somewhere the lamp shines.

For R we raised Fog's curve by 6 points everywhere and kept Fog as S. Under the page's six lamps they differ by 4.0 to 4.7, and over 2,000 random lamps, smooth and spiky, the smallest difference was 3.2.

Looking identical is another matter. Raise Fog by half a point instead and the two differ by at most 0.4 under the page's six lamps, too little to see, yet no lamp can make them match exactly.

Exercise 3: Deep red on the shop shelf

Imagine that a touch of deep red only raises Pebble's curve: a smooth bump, 25 points high at 650 nm and about 20 nm wide, with the curve unchanged everywhere else. Under which lamp does the change show least?

Show the answer and the numbers

The shop light. Only 8% of its light between 400 and 700 nm lies between 630 and 700 nm, against 44% for the warm bulb, so there is little light near 650 nm for the extra red to send back. The change makes a difference of 0.9 under the shop light, below the 1 that is just noticeable side by side, against 3.1 in daylight and 5.7 under the warm bulb.

A twist: the smooth glow, with the same color as the shop light, shows the change clearly, at 4.5. The shop light misses it because of the shape of its light, not its color.

Exercise 4: A lamp with two lines

Imagine a lamp that gives out light at only two wavelengths: a blue line at 450 nm and an orange line at 600 nm. You wiggle Fog while it hides from this lamp, as in step 4. Which part of Fog's curve can never move?

Show the answer and the numbers

Its heights at 450 and 600 nm, and nothing else. All of this lamp's light is at those two wavelengths, so all your eye can learn about Fog under it is how much of each comes back: the height of Fog's curve at 450 nm and at 600 nm. Blue and orange light stir your three kinds of cone in different proportions, so no trade between the two can keep all three numbers. Both heights are pinned, and everything else is free.

This page tried 200 random wiggles that hide from this lamp. They moved Fog's curve by up to 17 points, while its heights at 450 and 600 nm stayed at 30% and 32%; under the warm bulb the same wiggles changed Fog's color by up to 28. Step 4's figure shows the one-line case: choose Sodium lamp, press Wiggle Fog, and the curve stays pinned at 589 nm.

A twist: give the lamp a third line, of a third color, and every height is still pinned; give it a fourth and they come loose. Your eye keeps only three numbers, so with four lines the curve can trade light between them without changing its color.

Exercise 5: One block on your screen

Under the shop light this page paints Pebble and Fog as one block of color. Is the light leaving your screen from the Pebble half the same as the light from the Fog half?

Show the answer and the numbers

Yes, exactly the same light, although the paints themselves send back very different light. Under the shop light Pebble and Fog give your eye the same three numbers, and three numbers are all your screen works from, so it makes both halves from the same mix of its three lights. In the model screen of the last step, which gives your cones exactly the paints' three numbers, that mix is 46% red, 35% green and 19% blue, as shares of the light's power.

So a screen can copy what your eye gets from two matching paints, but never the difference between their lights. To make Pebble and Fog split, this page has to change the lamp. See it in the last step's figure: choose Shop light and switch between Pebble and Fog. The light off the paint changes shape, while the screen's three bands do not move at all.

Where you meet it

  • Paint: a chip judged under a shop's lamps can look different on your wall under your own. Look at samples at home, by day and at night.
  • Clothes and furnishings: fabrics dyed with different dyes can match on the shop floor and split by the window. The textile trade's viewing booths exist largely for this.
  • Lighting: filament bulbs and daylight have smooth spectra, while fluorescent tubes and white LEDs give their light in peaks and bumps, so they judge colors differently.
  • Streets: under low-pressure sodium lamps, the deep orange-yellow kind, cars of different colors turn into lighter and darker shades of one orange. The pinker high-pressure sodium lamps spread their light wider and leave some color.
  • Screens: every color on a screen is a mix of three lights. When a photo looks right, the mix is a metamer of the real thing; when it does not, one reason is that a camera's three color filters are not your three cones, and colors more vivid than the screen's three lights cannot be matched at all.

What this page simplifies

  • One average eye. The page computes with the CIE 1931 standard observer, an agreed average. Real eyes differ a little, so a match can also break from one person to the next (observer metamerism). Pebble and Fog were designed to match under the shop light for three of the CIE's model eyes at once: the 1931 and 1964 standard observers and the 2015 observer, built on the CIE's estimates of how each kind of cone responds; for the large-field version of that 2015 observer they differ by 0.1. The wiggle holds the match only for the 1931 observer: for the 1964 one most of its shapes would no longer match, at times by more than 10.
  • Cone signals. The bars are an estimate made from that observer with the Hunt-Pointer-Estévez transform, not measurements of your cones.
  • Curves. Real paints are measured with instruments at more wavelengths. Here every curve passes through 13 handles and is held level below 400 and above 700 nm. Gloss and fluorescence are ignored.
  • Lamps. A and D65 are CIE standard illuminants, FL11 and LED-B2 are CIE illuminants for typical lamps, and none is a particular product. The sodium street lamp (one line at 589 nm), the smooth glow (Planck's law at 3999 K) and the screen (three bands) are models.
  • What you see. Your eye adjusts to the color of a lamp. This page keeps 40% of each lamp's tint, so a warm room still looks warm, and it lights every room equally brightly. Your own screen and room change the colors again; the numbers, which compare each paint with a white sheet under the same lamp, are the reference.
  • Bigger than life. Pebble and Fog were designed to differ by about 19 under the warm bulb and 30 in daylight, so the change shows on almost any screen and to almost every eye. On a real wall, a difference of 2 is already visible side by side.
  • Color vision differs. If yours differs from the average, the splits may look smaller to you. The numbers and the bars still show them, and Pebble and Fog also differ in lightness under the warm bulb and in daylight.

This page checks its own numbers each time it loads: the white points of its four CIE lamps, the 34 published test pairs of the CIEDE2000 formula, the match of Pebble and Fog for two of the CIE's observers and their splits, the wiggle, and every exercise answer.

About & making of

This page is an Opus cameo: it was built by a team of Claude Opus agents instead of the usual Sonnet team. Every color on it is computed in your browser from CIE tables: the 1931 standard observer and the CIE illuminants A, D65, FL11 and LED-B2. The sodium street lamp (one line at 589 nm), the smooth 3999 K glow and the screen's three color bands are modeled, and the page says so wherever they appear. Pebble and Fog were designed by a script that searched for the smoothest pair of curves that match exactly under the shop light for the CIE's 1931 and 1964 standard observers and its 2015 cone-based observer alike, and split by about 19 under the warm bulb, with a change in lightness and in yellow against blue, so that the split also shows for most color-blind visitors. Color differences use CIEDE2000, checked against the 34 published test pairs of Sharma, Wu and Dalal; the page re-checks its numbers each time it loads.

What is computed and what is modeled

Computed from CIE data, in your browser: every lamp's white point, every color, every difference and every cone signal, all with the 1931 observer, and what the page says about its lamps, such as the shop light's three narrow lines near 435, 545 and 610 nm and the fact that a smooth glow at 3999 K has its color (figure 5 shows both); the 1964 observer is used only to check that the match holds for it too. Modeled by this page: the sodium line, the smooth glow, the screen's three bands, the 13-handle paint curves, and how colors are shown (40% of each lamp's tint kept, equal brightness for every room, sRGB). CIE data are published by the CIE; this page embeds their 5 nm tables from 380 to 780 nm.
