How an orbit sweeps over the Earth

What the map shows

A satellite at 420 to 700 km circles the Earth in about 93 to 99 minutes, while the Earth turns beneath it, so every lap crosses a new strip of ground. The line traced by the spot directly below the satellite is its ground track.

Here the track is knitted. The map is a grid of one-degree squares called stitches. Each satellite, called a needle, knits every stitch within half a swath (the width of ground it can see) of the track. A pass is one visit of a needle over a stitch.

Height and tilt

  • Height (perigee) sets the height of the orbit's lowest point, its perigee, above the ground: 200 to 2,000 km. A higher orbit takes longer per lap (see Orbit time).
  • Tilt (inclination) is the angle between the orbit and the equator: 0 degrees hugs it, 90 crosses both poles. Up to 90 degrees, the track never gets farther north or south than the tilt itself, so the International Space Station's 51.6 degrees never reaches the polar caps.

Why the flat map lies

A flat map stretches the polar regions sideways: a stitch near a pole is drawn as wide as one on the equator, though it covers far less ground. The chips on the map show two scores: Planet covered adds up true surface area; Map looks covered counts every stitch equally. Beyond 60 degrees of latitude (the angle north or south of the equator) lies a third of the flat map but about 13 percent of the planet. Polar view draws every stitch at its true relative size.

Orbits worth trying

A sun-synchronous orbit turns once a year in step with the sun, so the satellite crosses the equator at the same local time every day. Earth's slight bulge does the turning, and about 98 degrees of tilt at 600 to 700 km fits. The Tune sun-sync button sets that tilt.

A repeat orbit retraces its track after a whole number of days. Tall ticks under the Height slider mark repeat heights whose tracks sit closer together than the swath, so the stitches tile without gaps. Landsat, a series of Earth-observing satellites, repeats its sun-synchronous track every 16 days, after 233 orbits.

What this is not

It is a toy, not a tracker: its tracks drift from real satellites over weeks. Coastlines are hand-simplified. Sources are in the About panel below.

About & making of

How it works

Up to three satellites fly a two-body orbit with the J2 correction: Earth's bulge slowly turns each orbit's plane and perigee. Each pass is painted into a one-degree grid of stitches as wide as the satellite's swath. Scores weigh every stitch by its true area, so polar holes that look huge on a flat map count for little. This is a toy model, not a tracker: tracks drift from real satellites over weeks, and drag, the Moon and the Sun's pull are not modelled.

What the sources say (as of 2026-10-11)

A sun-synchronous orbit needs its node to drift about 0.9856 degrees per day, which Earth's J2 bulge supplies; that takes about 98.2 degrees of tilt at 700 km (NASA primer) and about 97.8 degrees near 600 km (Skill-Lync). The International Space Station flies at 51.6 degrees inclination, so it never gets farther north or south than 51.6 degrees latitude, at roughly 370 to 460 km in orbits of 90 to 93 minutes; each pass lands about 22.9 degrees of longitude west of the last (NASA tutorial; the altitude range is from the ESA table). Landsat 4 to 9 fly a circular sun-synchronous orbit near 705 km on a 16-day repeat of 233 orbits, about 98.9 minutes per orbit, successive orbits' tracks about 24.7 degrees (2752 km) apart at the equator, with a design swath of 185 km (NASA, USGS).

Computed here

The model gives 97.79 degrees at 600 km and 98.19 degrees at 700 km, matching both sources. 233 evenly spaced tracks put neighbours about 172 km apart (40,075 km / 233), so a 185 km swath overlaps its neighbour by about 13 km (7 percent); the model's 233-in-16 repeat lands at about 699.6 km, 0.8 percent below Landsat's 705 km. The band beyond 60 degrees latitude is a third of a flat map but about 13 percent of the planet. The model shifts the station's track 23.4 to 23.8 degrees per orbit (370 to 460 km), a few percent more than the NASA tutorial's figure, which matches a slightly shorter orbit. The 63.4 degree tilt where the perigee stops turning comes from the same formula.

Reported, unconfirmed

As of 2026-10-11, per arXiv 2007.04341 (one search result): Molniya-type orbits use about 63.4 degrees of tilt, a 12-hour period and two apogees a day.

Made up or hand-made

Coastlines are hand-simplified outlines and city dots are approximate; there are no borders. Pars were found by brute-force search with this model. Card names, thresholds and yarn colours are the team's. The sun moves uniformly along the ecliptic (no equation of time).
