How painting out a sound works

Reading the picture

A spectrogram is a picture of sound. Time runs from left to right, pitch rises from bottom to top, and the brighter a spot, the louder that pitch is at that moment. A played note shows up as a stack of short lines: the lowest is the pitch you hear, and the fainter lines above it are its overtones, which give each instrument its colour. In the demo clip, the music-box notes are short fading dashes and the held chord is a band of steady lines near the bottom.

Noises have shapes of their own. A cough is a bright cloud that covers most pitches for about a third of a second. A click is a thin upright line, because it is very short and contains every pitch at once. A beep or a phone ping is one bright line, or two. That is why painting works: in the picture, the noise and the music usually sit in different places, even when you hear them at the same moment.

Erase or Fill in

Erase turns the painted part down by 60 decibels, which leaves a thousandth of its strength. (Decibels, dB for short, measure sound level: every 20 dB makes the wave ten times smaller.) Erase suits a noise that nothing you want shares, such as a ping over room tone, the faint hiss of a quiet room. Over music it leaves a hole: on the demo clip's cough, Erase also takes away the chord underneath, and that stretch goes silent.

Fill in rebuilds the painted part instead. In every pitch band it looks at about 46 milliseconds of sound just before and just after the painted area and blends from one side to the other. Where the music under the noise was louder than the noise, it starts from that sound's own timing (its phase, explained below) instead of guessing; where a band held only steady hiss, it adds a matching flicker so the patch does not sound frozen.

On the demo clip, the difference between the filled-in recording and a version that never had the cough is 20.7 dB quieter than the music itself; with Erase, the difference is as loud as the music. The number under the picture after a Fill in stroke is a different measure: how much quieter the sound became where the noise was. On the demo clip the page knows exactly where each noise was, because it made them; elsewhere it counts the painted spots that changed by at least 3 dB. The 20.7 dB check needs a copy of the recording without the noise, which only the demo clip has, so it appears here and in the checks at the end.

From picture back to sound

A digital recording is a long list of numbers, 44,100 for every second of sound in the demo clip; each number, called a sample, says how far the air was pushed at that instant. To draw the picture, the page cuts this list into overlapping slices of 2,048 samples (46 milliseconds), each starting 512 samples after the one before. Each slice is shaped by a smooth hump called a Hann window and measured with the Fourier transform, which says how much of each of 1,025 frequency bands the slice contains, 21.5 hertz apart. (Frequency is how fast the air vibrates, which you hear as pitch; one hertz, Hz, is one vibration per second, and 1 kHz is a thousand.) This slicing is called the short-time Fourier transform, and adding the slices back up is called overlap-add.

The humps are shaped so that the overlapping slices, squared and added, total exactly 1.5 everywhere; dividing by 1.5 then returns an untouched recording within one part in a billion. The page rebuilds only the slices you painted and leaves every other sample alone, so a stroke on a two-minute file takes about as long as one on ten seconds.

Each frequency in a slice also has a phase (where its wave is in its cycle), and invented phases can clash where slices overlap. The page carries each phase on from its neighbours, then runs up to 12 rounds of a method Daniel Griffin and Jae Lim published in 1984, which nudges the rebuilt slices until they agree.

Where it struggles

Fill in only knows what came just before and just after the painted area. It works best on short noises over steady sound: a cough over a held chord, a click between notes, a ping over room tone. Some cases it cannot fix:

  • Over continuous speech, Fill in usually leaves a cough in place, because the words carry on past it, and the page says so; Erase turns the cough down but takes the words under it too. When the engine is made to fill anyway, the words come back blurred and about 14 dB too quiet, because nothing at the edges says what was said. A cough between words works (next point).
  • A cough between words needs a moment of quiet on each side, about a sixth of a second. Closer to the words, Fill in smears the word endings into the gap, and Erase is the better choice: in our test with 75 milliseconds of quiet, Erase took the cough 32 dB down and Fill in about 16.
  • Long fills over changing notes smear. Across 0.7 seconds of a moving melody, the notes inside the painted area turn into a blur of the ones on either side.
  • A beep that crosses a melody can leave a duller, quieter stretch (about 3 dB quieter in that pitch band in our test), because when a note starts or stops under the paint, the page has to guess the moment from the noisy sound there.
  • A sound that carries on past your stroke is rebuilt, not removed. When a stroke up to 0.8 seconds wide would clearly take it all out, as with a cough, the page widens the stroke and says so; otherwise paint over all of it, or use Erase. The page warns you when it can tell.

This is classic signal processing, not machine learning. It cannot make up new words or a new tune; at worst it smears the sound from either side across the gap. When a fill sounds wrong, press Undo, or choose Removed on the switch to hear exactly what went.

The practical limits: recordings up to 2 minutes long (a longer file is cut to its first 2 minutes); one or two channels (extra channels are dropped); WAV files (standard uncompressed sound files) keep their own sample rate, the number of samples per second, while MP3, Ogg, FLAC and other formats are converted to 48,000 samples per second; M4A opens in most browsers but not all; from a video only the sound is used; one painted area applies to both channels of a stereo file; and the result is saved as a WAV file with 16-bit samples, the precision of a CD.

Why this is free here

As of October 2026, the free audio editors we checked can mute a marked area of the picture, and some can patch a click a few milliseconds long. Rebuilding a longer stretch, such as a cough, was a paid feature in every ready-to-use editor we found; the one free project we found that tries it must be installed and run from code. This page does both, in your browser, with nothing to install. Your recording is never uploaded, and the page keeps working offline once it has loaded.

Checks this page runs on itself

The button below (also under More) runs the page's own code against known answers. Some come from textbooks: a single click spreads equally over every pitch, a pure tone lands in one frequency band, the Fourier transform keeps the energy and matches a slow, direct calculation, and Hann windows add up to exactly 1 and 1.5. Others come from the WAV format, such as one second of mono 16-bit sound at 44,100 samples per second making an 88,244-byte file. The rest measure this page and say so, such as Erase making a tone 60 dB quieter and Fill in bringing back a steady tone within 1 dB, even at the very start of a file. In the list, DFT and FFT are the Fourier transform, STFT is its sliced form, a bin is one frequency band, N is the slice length and the hop is the step from one slice to the next; PCM is the plain number format inside a WAV file, and ByteRate and BlockAlign are two fields of its header.

About & making of

This page is an Opus cameo: it was built by a team of Claude Opus agents instead of the usual Sonnet team. Everything runs in your browser. A hand-written Fourier transform in a background worker turns the recording into a picture, your strokes become a soft-edged mask, and only the slices you painted are rebuilt and added back, so the rest of the file stays bit for bit the same. Fill in rebuilds each frequency band from about 46 ms of sound on each side, starts from the measured phase wherever the music is louder than the noise, adds matching flicker to steady hiss, and settles the result with up to 12 rounds of the Griffin-Lim method.
